Tutor Me
Overview

Finding a tutor in India still runs on WhatsApp forwards, coaching-centre boards and a phone number written on the back of a receipt. Pricing is opaque, verification is a leap of faith, and rescheduling means a call. Tutor Me is my concept for the alternative — a learner says what they want to work on, browses tutors who teach it filtered by rating, price, availability and language, and books a session without leaving the app.

The scope explored here is the first-run experience: signup, discovery, tutor profile, and the booking + learning hub. It's tuned for the Indian market — rupee pricing, IST scheduling, English + Hindi tutors, and space in the interface for the WhatsApp-shaped rituals students already use around tuition.

The problem

Finding a tutor is easy. Finding the one is not.

WhatsApp forwards, coaching-centre boards, a number on the back of a receipt. Opaque pricing, no verification, no way to reschedule.

Names are not scarce — everyone knows someone who knows a tutor. What’s missing is any way to tell them apart: no rate you can see, no history you can check, no signal beyond who recommended them.

The gap I set out to design for is the consumer-grade mobile experience — booking a tutor should feel closer to picking a ride than to filing a form.

Storyboard

Why finding a tutor takes a week.

Goals

Five things the design had to do.

01

Get to a tutor in under a minute.

Every screen in signup and discovery is scored against this one metric. If a step doesn't earn its place, cut it.

02

Make trust visible, not stated.

Verification badges, rating counts, honest empty states, response times — surface the signals people already look for, don't make them read a paragraph.

03

Give 1:1 and Batch equal weight.

Indian tutoring runs on both private tuition and group batches. The product treats them as first-class equals, not as a hidden setting.

04

Design for the second session.

Bookings, messages, and rebooking flows are built so the follow-through is easier than the first-time browse. That's what makes a marketplace stick.

05

Sell the session, not the month.

A learner books one day, three days, or a full week — whatever the gap actually needs. Paying for a month to fix two topics is the waste that keeps people away from tuition in the first place.

User research

Understanding how people actually find a tutor.

Class 12 JEE aspirant · Guwahati

1. What's the hardest part of finding a tutor?

Two weeks of looking and still not booked. Friends, family, and scrolling social media — no single place to search.

2. How did you find the names you considered?

Friends and family, plus social media. Not search, not an app, not a directory.

3. How did you decide between them?

“Word of mouth, but really confusing.” No way to compare one recommendation against another.

4. What do you judge a tutor on?

How many students they’ve taught, and their experience — which he treats as roughly the same signal as ratings and review count.

5. Why did it take two weeks?

Not asked directly. The answer implied by the rest: finding offline is a hassle, and there is nowhere to look that shows everything at once.

6. What would you change about how tuition is sold?

“Even if I have to study only two topics, I have to pay monthly charges.” Offline tuition is sold by the month and by batch, so choosing wrong is expensive.

UrbanPro
India · tutor marketplace
The closest real comparison. A directory of individual tutors — reported at 500,000+ verified tutors across 1,000+ categories. Students post an enquiry; matching tutors see it.
Lead generation, not commission. Tutors buy coins to unlock a student's contact details, refunded if the enquiry converts.

Not a marketplace. Subscription access to live classes from named educators, plus a recorded library; 1:1 mentorship only in the top tier.

PhysicsWallah
India · low-cost batches
Also not a marketplace. Structured batches for grades 6–12 and JEE/NEET, built for volume and reach into Tier II and III cities.
Price as the wedge. Online courses typically ₹3,000–₹10,000 a year, JEE/NEET batches from ₹2,999 — against ₹50,000–₹1.5L at legacy offline coaching.
A batch can't adapt to one student's gaps. Tutor Me treats 1:1 and batch as equal booking types, so the learner chooses the format.

Three findings from the scan.

01

India's biggest names sell content, not tutors.

Unacademy and PhysicsWallah dominate mindshare, but both sell access to classes. Neither lets a learner browse individual tutors and book one. Vedantu is the notable exception with structured 1:1 as a core offering.

02

The one real marketplace stops at the introduction.

UrbanPro's coin model pays it to hand over a phone number — so the product has little reason to own scheduling, payment or the second session. That's precisely where the design work is.

03

Batch and 1:1 are equals in Indian tuition.

Group batches and private tuition run side by side here, and PhysicsWallah's scale is evidence of it. Treating batch as a first-class booking type — not a hidden variant — is an India-shaped decision.

Public sources, September 2026 — pricing pages, help documentation and press coverage. No primary user research or proprietary data.

User flow

The booking path, at a glance.

Key screens · Chapter one

Onboarding — six screens, one line.

01 · Welcome

A single door with two doorknobs.

One obvious way in for new users, and a quieter "Sign in" for people coming back. A small badge shows how many tutors are online.

One loud thing per screen. The start button is the brightest element here. Everything after it is calmer, so this stays the obvious next step.
Proof you glance at, not read. "12,400 tutors online" is a badge, not a paragraph of testimonials.
Intent before credentials. No password or phone number yet — the first question is what you need help with.
02 · Role

Ask the fork question early.

Learner or Tutor — the answer branches everything downstream. Learner is pre-selected as the most common path; each card carries a one-line "what you'll get" instead of an icon-only guess.

Reassurance copy under the header. "You can switch roles or do both later" defuses the fear of picking wrong.
Progress dots, not a numeric counter. Four dots communicate "you're near the start" without giving the user an odometer to resent.
03 · Account

Three fields, then a shortcut.

Name, email, phone — nothing else asked for. Social sign-in sits below the divider for people who would rather not type at all.

Say what the phone number is for. "We'll text a 6-digit code to verify" answers the question before the field can raise it.
Terms above the button, not after. What you are agreeing to is stated before the tap, not confirmed by it.
Social sign-in second. The form stays the honest default; Google and Apple sit where a shortcut belongs, under the divider.
04 · Verify OTP

Six cells and one honest timer.

The OTP field surfaces one active cell at a time with a ring, and an info banner tells the user exactly when the "Resend" affordance below it will actually do anything. No hidden state, no double-tap ambiguity.

Editable "Change" number. When the SMS fails — and it will — the recovery is a tap, not a return to the top of the funnel.
Primary button disabled until valid. The button visibly changes state; it never lies about being ready.
05 · Subjects & goal

Prefer chips over dropdowns for anything the user browses.

Subjects live as tappable pills — same anatomy across the whole app, from onboarding to search filters. A goal question lives directly below, giving matching an intent signal without extending the flow.

Pre-selected defaults. A few common subjects are pre-selected as an editable starting point; the user shapes it in seconds instead of building from zero.
Goal as radio, not free text. Structured answers feed the matching algorithm; free text would just sit unread in a database.
06 · You're in

The last screen of onboarding is the first screen of the product.

A success moment that pays back the six preceding taps with a real, matched list — not a stock illustration. The user leaves onboarding with a tutor already visible.

Personalised confirmation. The greeting uses the name entered on step 03; the count uses the subjects picked on step 05.
TOP tag as the only decoration. Every other visual signal on the tutor rows is data.
Two exits, one intent. "See all" enters the tutor list; "Go to home" seeds the Home tab — both good outcomes.
Key screens · Chapter two

Discovery, and the profile that has to close the sale.

Home is the everyday surface. Tutor detail is the moment of decision — with a booking bar that never leaves the screen.

07 · Home

Search where they land, browse when they scroll.

Home is the longest screen in the product, so it is shown in the three bands a learner actually scrolls through. Search anchors the top for people who know what they want; everything below it is for people who don't yet.

Tutor card as an object. The same card — avatar, rating, subjects, rate, Book — appears on Home, in search results and in recommendations, so it is learned once.
The offer makes a claim, not a decoration. "Free first session this week" states what the user gets and when it ends, instead of a banner that only fills space.
Three ways down one screen. Recommended, subject explorer and live sessions each catch a different intent, so a scroll always lands on something actionable.
09 · Tutor detail

The sale happens on the sticky bar.

Everything above it — badges, rating, expertise chips, this week's availability — is written to answer the objections that stop a booking. The bar itself never leaves the screen, and it carries the price so the user is never surprised at checkout.

Rupee-first pricing. ₹349/hr renders as the anchor number; there's no dollar-to-INR translation happening in the user's head.
Slot counters on days. "3 slots" under a day cell is a stronger booking prompt than "available" — scarcity, honestly represented.
Both class types announced. "1:1 & Batch" appears as a fact under the tutor, before the booking flow forks below.

11 · Bookings

The next session is the whole point.

A single "NEXT UP" card, gradient-topped, with a live countdown and a Join Call CTA — the rest of the week sits below the fold. Stats up top let a returning user feel their own progress before they scroll.

12 · Messages

Support is a message, not a form.

Tutor Me's own messages sit in the same inbox as tutor threads, badged as SUPPORT. Read receipts, typing states, and pinned conversations behave the way iOS users already expect from Messages and WhatsApp.

13 · Profile

The dashboard is the identity.

A floating stats card overlaps the cobalt hero; grouped settings sit below with tinted icons that reuse the palette. Every row shows its current value so the user doesn't have to tap to check.

Key UX decisions

Five decisions that shaped the product.

Problem → Decision → Benefit

Success screen becomes the first product screen.

Problem
Onboarding usually ends on an empty pat-on-the-back — the highest-intent moment gets wasted.
Decision
"You're in" lands with real matched tutors already visible, plus a "See all" and "Go to home" — two live exits.
Benefit
The learner leaves onboarding with a tutor already picked out. First booking becomes one screen away, not eight.
Problem → Decision → Benefit

Tutor cards do the same job in three places.

Problem
Marketplaces reinvent their tutor card on every surface; users have to relearn every screen.
Decision
One card anatomy — avatar, distance, rating, name, tags, price, BOOK — used on Home rail, Success list, and Tutor list.
Benefit
The card becomes a mental object, not a layout. Users can compare tutors between surfaces without translation.
Problem → Decision → Benefit

"No reviews yet" beats a fake five stars.

Problem
New tutors on marketplaces are usually shown with a five-star row that reads as zero and quietly kills their bookings.
Decision
New tutors carry a muted star and the italic phrase "No reviews yet" — an honest empty state.
Benefit
The user's trust in every other rating on the page goes up. The new tutor gets a fair shot rather than a false negative.
Problem → Decision → Benefit

Availability lives on the tutor's profile as slots-per-day.

Problem
"Available Mon–Fri" is a spec-sheet answer; users want to know "can I get in this week."
Decision
A 7-day strip on the tutor profile with a slot count under each day — "3 slots", or muted with a dash for full days.
Benefit
The user picks the day before they open the booking flow. Scarcity is honest, not manufactured.
Problem → Decision → Benefit

1:1 and Batch travel side-by-side, not one hidden under the other.

Problem
Indian tuition splits about half-and-half between private and batch classes; forcing one to be a sub-option under the other misreads the market.
Decision
Tutor profile announces "1:1 & Batch" as a fact-tile; the booking bar routes into either flow with a segmented switch.
Benefit
The user chooses the commitment shape before the schedule shape — the correct order for a category with two very different mental models.
Problem → Decision → Benefit

The bottom bar is where every commit happens.

Problem
Scrolling a long tutor profile or booking review shouldn't bury the CTA at the bottom of the page.
Decision
Every screen with a commit action has a persistent bottom bar carrying the price and the primary action, disabled until the state is valid.
Benefit
The user is never more than a thumb-flick away from "book" — and never lied to about what happens when they tap it.

Trade-offs & priorities

What I chose, and what I gave up to choose it.

+

I prioritised

Where the effort went
The learner's first booking
Signup through confirmation is designed end to end, because it's the path that decides whether the product is ever used twice.
Trust you can see
Verification, rating counts, response times and honest empty states sit on the card itself, not in a policy page.
1:1 and Batch as equals
Both are first-class booking types rather than a toggle, because Indian tuition genuinely runs on both.
A system small enough to hold
Eight tokens, about ten components. Restraint now is what makes the tutor side extendable later.
−

I let go of

Deliberate, not overlooked
The tutor-side product
Tutor appears in the role picker but has no flow behind it. A marketplace is two products; only one is designed here.
Payments beyond a single confirm
No wallet, refunds, receipts or failure states. Enough to complete a booking, not enough to run one.
Tablet and web

Multi-session packages were cut. They need tutor supply and retention data that a concept round can't produce.
Learnings

What Tutor Me taught me.

01

A marketplace is two products.

Designing just the learner surface makes for a good catalog. Making it a marketplace means the tutor product exists too — verification, availability, payouts, safety. Even before those screens shipped, the learner-side design had to leave room for them.

02

Restraint compounds.

Eight colours, one font, one card, one chip. Every time I was tempted to add a variant, I asked whether the existing component could do the job. It almost always could — and the app got calmer for it.

03

Empty states are where trust is built.

"No reviews yet" was one line of italic text. It's the moment I stopped designing for the demo and started designing for the day the product actually exists in the world.

04

Not designing something is a design decision.

Learning Plans (recurring 1:1 across weeks) was the feature I most wanted to design. Sitting with it long enough, I could see the operational and refund risk it would carry in a concept-stage marketplace. The right decision was to shape the hooks (BookingGroup, plan segment on the profile) and defer the surface itself. That patience is a design skill.

05

Local details are not decoration.

Rupee pricing, Mon–Sat weeks, "Batch" as a first-class word, WhatsApp-shaped rituals — designing for the Indian market means letting the market's vocabulary into the interface, not translating a Western template into it.

Next steps

Where this would go, if it were built.

If the concept were picked up as a real product, these five moves are what I’d design next.

01

Design the tutor-side onboarding.

The role picker offers "I'm a tutor" but the flow beyond it is a placeholder — the whole file today is learner-side. A marketplace is two products in a trench coat; the tutor one needs identity verification, calendar setup, rate suggestion, payout onboarding, and a first-90-days education layer to reduce churn.

Round 2 · foundational
02

Extend payment into wallet, refunds, and receipts.

The transactional payment states — successful, processing, failed — are already designed. What's still open is the long tail: add-payment-method sheet, wallet credit + top-up, refund policy screen, invoices, and the EMI split-pay picker (unlocked once ticket size crosses ₹3k for batch enrolments).

Round 2 · foundational
03

Fill the remaining edge-state gaps.

Payment success/processing/failed and empty-search are designed; still missing across the file: OTP-mismatch error, offline banner, empty Bookings, empty Messages, list-loading skeletons, and search-failure retry. Small screens each, but they're what separates a design demo from a shippable product.

Round 2 · foundational
04

Ship the "Book again, same time next week" pattern.

Small surface, disproportionate leverage. When a session ends, a sheet offers the same tutor, the same slot, next week — one tap, no new search, no re-deciding. It works on the moment the intent already exists, instead of asking the learner to rebuild it from Home a week later. Deliberately lighter than a Learning Plan: a repeat booking, not a commitment, so a learner can fall into a weekly rhythm without signing up for a term. This is also the feature the second-session rate in the validation plan would directly test — if learners rebook but mostly with a different tutor, the pattern is wrong and discovery deserves the effort instead.

Round 3 · retention
05

Introduce Learning Plans — when the supply is ready.

The concept is designed (multi-week recurring 1:1 with monthly billing, curriculum, pause + reschedule credits) but not built. Revisit when tutor supply is proven, cancellation rate is low, and users have started asking for it unprompted. Data model already accommodates it.
